Steering looped language models · interactive draft

Later loops undo steering

A looped language model runs every token through the same stack of layers several times. Steer it on an early loop and the loops that follow undo the edit. Steer the last loop, or bake the edit into the weights, and it sticks, except in one model, where the weight edit wrecks everything.

In all three looped models, erasing the refusal direction on the first loop barely matters, because the later loops bring refusal back. Erasing it on the last loop alone does most of the job.

A dense model behaves differently. Qwen3-8B ignores edits to its first and last quarter and collapses when a middle quarter is edited.

Persona vectors and random kicks show the same pattern. Weight edits act on every loop, so no later loop can undo them. They abliterate two looped models cleanly and break the third.

Setup

Same layers, run again

A standard transformer passes each token through its layers once. A looped model has a shorter stack and runs each token's state through it several times, with the same weights every time. A loop is not a new thought or a new token: it is the same token's state, processed again by the same layers.

In the drawings below, each loop of the tube is one loop of the model. The three looped models differ in how one loop hands over to the next and in where they normalise the stream. Dory adds its input back before every loop and keeps the whole stream at unit length. Nanbeige and Ouro pass the previous loop's output through a shared norm. Every model, Qwen included, normalises what each layer reads. Under each drawing: how one layer updates the stream h, and what happens between loops.

We use the standard steering tools. The refusal direction is the difference in mean activation between prompts the model refuses and prompts it answers. We either erase it from the residual stream at every layer (directional ablation) or add its opposite at one layer (activation addition). In a looped model we can apply either one on any subset of loops. In Qwen we apply it to a subset of quarters.

Experiment 1 · looped vs dense

Last loop, or middle layers

Erase refusal on one stretch of the forward pass at a time and the looped models disagree with the dense one about where it matters. In Qwen the middle quarters do almost everything and its first and last quarters do little. In a looped model the first loop is the weakest place to erase, and the last loop alone does most of the job.

Where one stretch of erasing matters

Refusal rate after erasing the refusal direction on one stretch only, drawn over that stretch of the forward pass: red is the share of harmful prompts still refused, blue the share answered. Dory runs 4 loops here, two more than it was trained with; every model at its trained loops is in the panels below. Each of Qwen's quarters is drawn as wide as one of Dory's loops, so its 36 layers line up under Dory's looped stack. Dashed: no steering. Hover a column for its 95% interval.

The two shapes have different causes. In Qwen every layer is different. The middle layers build the refusal signal and the later ones act on it, so an erase in the first quarter is rebuilt by the middle, and by the last quarter the answer no longer depends much on the direction. In a looped model the late loops are the same layers as the early ones. Erase refusal on loop 1 and loop 2 builds it again with the same weights; erase it on the last loop and nothing comes after to rebuild it.

Experiment 4 · extra loops

Run two more loops

If later loops undo early steering, extra loops should undo more. Steer every loop a model was trained with, then let the same model run its shared layers two more times, with no steering, before it answers. Nothing about the model changes; it only runs longer. We repeat the three experiments this way: refusal, personas and random kicks.

Dory heals completely in every experiment. Ouro recovers from random kicks with two extra loops but not from refusal steering. Nanbeige cannot be tested this way, since beyond two loops it produces gibberish even without steering.

Synthesis

So do looped models self-correct?

Yes, in a specific sense: later loops restore behaviour that an intervention on an earlier loop removed. The scoreboard below puts all three experiments side by side. Each bar is how much of the full effect survives when only that loop is steered.

Personas: trait gain divided by the gain when every loop is steered. Dashed line in each cell: 100%, the effect of steering every loop. Dory runs 4 loops here, two more than it was trained with, so each of its bars has up to three loops after it. Looped models rise towards the last loop; Qwen peaks in the middle.

It is not a defence. Steer the last loop, or every loop, and all three models fall the way a dense model does. Dense models also recompute an erased direction in their later layers. What they lack is a second pass through the layers that read it.

Why · the first suspect

Is it the re-injected input?

Why would a later loop rebuild what an earlier one lost? Start with the model that does it most. Dory recovers most strongly, and it is the one model that adds its input back before every loop (the shortcut under its loops). That makes the copy a natural suspect: a later loop could rebuild refusal by re-reading the prompt as the entry stack saw it. Ouro and Nanbeige have no copy; each loop only gets the previous loop's output.

We tested this on Dory run with four loops. Erase the refusal direction on loops 1 and 2, change what is added back before loops 3 and 4, and measure the refusal signal the stream carries at every sublayer. The reasoning traces are fixed (teacher-forced), so this measures the stream rather than new behaviour. Here the stream's colour follows that measured signal.

The models without a copy point the same way: Ouro and Nanbeige still undo and of a first-loop erase. So the comeback does not need re-injection, and it does not need refusal to be copied forward. The likelier source is the loops themselves: they work out again that the request should be refused, and write the refusal direction anew.

What this does not rule out: the copy still carries what the prompt says, and a loop could work out refusal from that, just as it can from the carried state. Removing that content means removing the copy, which breaks the model, so this test cannot tell the two apart.

Why · our reading

The answer settles in the first loop

If the loops work refusal out again, why would they? Our reading starts from one measurement: read the model's guess out of the stream after each loop, using its own output head (a logit lens), and compare that guess with the next token it finally predicts. We do this over clean reasoning traces, with no steering.

A looped model has a usable answer after its first loop; a dense model has none until its last layers. So the later loops of a looped model mostly take a state that is already close to its answer and run the same layers over it again. Whatever computed refusal on loop 1 sees almost the same input on loop 2, and computes it again. On this reading, self-correction needs no repair mechanism: it is the same function applied to a state that has nearly settled, and an erase on an early loop is a small push that the next pass undoes.

If that is right, the more settled a model is after loop 1, the more of a loop-1 erase it should undo. The three looped models line up that way (predictions final after loop 1, then share of a loop-1 erase undone): Nanbeige , Ouro , Dory .

Qwen is left out. Its first quarter has almost no answer yet (1%), and erasing there does little for a different reason: refusal has not formed yet, and its middle layers build it from scratch.

Three models that differ in many ways, so this is an association, not a test. It fits the other results: Ouro, the least settled after loop 1 of the four-loop runs, is also the one whose extra loops do not bring refusal back.

Part 2 · weight edits

Bake it into the weights

Hooks need code that runs during inference. The usual attack on open weights bakes the edit into the model instead: project the refusal direction out of every matrix that writes to the residual stream (weight orthogonalization, or abliteration). In a looped model that edit is in place on every loop, like a kick that repeats each time round.

Qwen, Nanbeige and Dory stop refusing and keep their maths. Ouro's standard edit wipes out its maths, and the same happens with a random direction, so the damage has nothing to do with refusal. A norm-aware edit brings refusal down to but costs nearly half of MATH-500 and most of MMLU-Pro. Hooks still remove Ouro's refusal at no cost, so it resists a recipe, not an attacker who can run code.

Part 2 · mechanism

Why Ouro breaks

A weight edit projects the refusal direction u out of every matrix that writes to the stream, so no write has anything along u. In a pre-norm model like Qwen or Nanbeige that settles it: each write is added to the stream as it is. Ouro normalises every write before adding it (the second ring of each pair on its card), and a norm multiplies every dimension by its own learned gain. Gains that differ from one dimension to the next turn vectors, so a write with nothing along u comes out of the norm with something along it.

How a norm puts an erased direction back

Two dimensions instead of 2048. The write x (blue) has nothing along u. The norm rescales each dimension by its gain, which stretches the dashed circle into the ellipse and turns x towards u; the red stretch is what the output now carries along u. With equal gains, g ⊙ u points along u and nothing leaks. A hook removes u after the norm instead, so nothing is left to turn.

Ouro's write norms turn u by about 18° on average (the cosine between u and g ⊙ u is 0.95; 37° for the worst one). Each write leaks only a little back, but a four-loop pass makes 192 writes. The writers cannot cancel what leaked, because the edit stopped them writing along u at all, and the unedited model does write along u to keep it in check. Then the shared norm between loops turns and rescales the stream again, so the leak compounds from loop to loop.

  • Leak 1, the write norms. The edited matrices write almost nothing along u (0.001), but the norm after each one rotates some back: 0.09 to 0.17 per write. This alone breaks Ouro, even when the edited direction is random: its replies fall apart with fewer loops too.
  • Leak 2, the shared norm between loops. It multiplies whatever leaked by 2 to 3 every loop. A norm-aware edit removes g ⊙ u from each writer instead of u, which closes leak 1 exactly, yet the stream still ends loop 4 at 4.0× the unedited model, and the model loses nearly half its MATH-500 score.
  • The other two models. Nanbeige has no norm on its writes and its shared norm shrinks the stream, so neither leak applies (0.16× after loop 2). Dory has no gains, but its per-dimension update rate α plays the same role; its standard edit is weaker than its hooks (26% refusal against 13%), and correcting for α makes it weaker still (62%). Its leftover leak is not explained yet.
Takeaways

Design levers

Each looped model bundles several design choices, so most of this is association across three models. Two choices were tested directly: Ouro's norms explain why its weight edit fails, and Dory's re-injected input does not explain its comeback.

What to do with this

  • For interpretability: probe, patch and steer the last loop first. A null result on an early loop says little about the model.
  • For safety evaluations: none of this stops an attacker who hooks the last loop. A model that resists one weight-editing recipe is not tamper resistant. Fine-tuning attacks are untested here.
  • For architecture: self-correction appears with and without re-injecting the input, and stripping the refusal information from Dory's copy does not stop it. Norm placement decides whether naive weight edits survive.
Fine print

Methods and limits

  • Refusal. 487 harmful test prompts (250 for some loop subsets), 3 reasoning traces per prompt and 3 answers per trace at temperature 0.6, each answer judged by gpt-oss-20b with the StrongREJECT rubric. Unfinished traces are judged whole. Intervals resample prompts.
  • Directions. One direction per model, extracted at one site and reused everywhere: Ouro loop 4 layer 16, Nanbeige loop 2 layer 15, Dory loop 1 sublayer 26 (harmful minus harmless prompts), Qwen layer 21.
  • Kicks. A random unit direction per problem, times a fraction of the state's norm, added at each loop's middle layer. 100 MATH-500 problems, one sample each, reasoning up to 8192 tokens.
  • Personas. Persona-vector method of Chen et al. (2025), 20 questions × 5 samples per setting, trait and coherence judged by gpt-oss-20b. Strengths tuned per model so coherence stays within 10 points of clean.
  • Limits. Single runs. One judge with no human calibration. Extra-loop tests run models past their trained loop count. Three looped models, each with several design differences. Leak mechanism on 8 traces.
  • Concurrent work. A LessWrong post on Ouro (24 Sep 2026, "A recurrent LLM is quite easy to interpret but complex to steer") also finds steering works best on the last loop. arXiv 2610.15218 and arXiv 2610.10625 link refusal and jailbreaks to the number of loops.
